01 / Text-to-video plan

A pencil rolls across a desk.

The purpose is a quiet opening shot for a study video. The viewer should notice the pencil coming to rest, with no second event competing for attention.

Starting idea

“A pencil rolls across a desk. Keep the camera still but orbit around it. A hand catches it, then the notebook opens. Make it cinematic. Six seconds.”

Make the decisions explicit

  1. Choose the event. Keep the pencil rolling to a stop. Remove the hand and opening notebook: those are additional actions with their own timing. They can become later shots.
  2. Resolve the camera conflict. Use a fixed, slightly elevated view. A camera cannot stay fixed and orbit in the same shot; the fixed view makes the pencil’s motion easier to inspect.
  3. Give the motion a beginning and an end. Begin with the pencil already moving. End with it at rest, clearly short of the notebook. No unexplained hand needs to enter the frame.
  4. Replace the mood word with visible choices. Use soft window light, a plain wooden desk and a closed notebook. Leave the background still.

Revised shot brief

A six-second, fixed-camera close shot from a slightly elevated angle. One unmarked yellow pencil is already rolling slowly from left to right across a wooden desk. A closed notebook sits on the right. The pencil slows and stops with a visible gap before the notebook; hold that final state for the last second. Soft window light and a still background. Keep the whole pencil in frame, with its shape and color unchanged. No hand enters and the notebook stays closed.

Start
Pencil already rolling; notebook visible.
Middle
Pencil slows; camera and notebook stay still.
End
Pencil at rest with a gap before the notebook.

A different choice, with a cost

If a moving camera matters more than the rolling action, keep the pencil still and use a slow lateral camera move. That emphasizes the desk’s depth, but loses the small event this opening shot was meant to show. Change the brief’s purpose with the choice.

What to inspect later

If you later generate or film a draft, check whether the pencil stays whole, the camera remains fixed, and the ending is visible. The requested stop is a planning intention, not a guarantee of model behavior.

One revision to try: if the pencil reaches the notebook before it stops, shorten the requested travel distance while keeping the camera and other constraints unchanged. Compare the ending before changing another variable; this revision has not been tested.

02 / Image-to-video plan

A front photo cannot specify the back.

Assume you have a straight-on photograph of a plain blue product box. Only its front is visible. The purpose is a restrained close-up that preserves the photographed face.

Starting idea

“Use this photo. Orbit all the way around the box, show its back, and keep every surface exactly as photographed.”

The conflict: the photo does not show the back or sides. “Exactly as photographed” cannot specify those unseen surfaces. An orbit would require information the reference does not contain.

The decision: keep the front-facing view and use a small push-in. The box stays still; the camera supplies the only motion. Frame it with space around the edges so the entire front remains visible at the end.

Revised shot brief

A five-second close-up using the supplied front photograph of the blue box. Keep the box stationary and facing straight toward the camera. Make a small, slow push-in without rotating around the box or revealing new surfaces. Preserve the visible front’s proportions, color and existing markings; keep the background and light unchanged. End with the entire front face still inside the frame.

The alternative: if showing the back is essential, collect a rear reference and plan a separate rear-view shot. That adds reference work and an edit, but avoids pretending the front photo documents the back.

Check and revise: if a later draft crops an edge, reduce only the requested push-in. Inspect visible markings separately; this brief does not promise exact text or product fidelity. A still-image move in an editor is another option if preserving the original pixels is the priority.

The written plan is ready when…

  • You can point to one main action and one consistent camera plan.
  • The starting and final states are different in a specific, visible way.
  • Every “keep unchanged” instruction agrees with the requested action and available reference.
  • Someone else can describe the intended shot without guessing what “cinematic” means.

This checks the written plan. A generated or filmed result still needs its own review.
